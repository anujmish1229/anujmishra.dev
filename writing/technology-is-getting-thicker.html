Technology is getting thicker, and it's not great

An indictment of big tech's "premium-ization" of devices by making them thicker, furthering the anti-consumer practices from these brands.

July 11, 2025, 5 min read

Whenever I make snacks, I always love eating the ones that are slightly malformed. They taste so much better in my mind, even though they are the same as the correctly formed ones. Same ingredients, maybe slightly different cooking methods, but that isn't what makes the impact. We are naturally drawn to flaws in what we like. If something were perfect, we would very quickly and easily get bored of it. Big tech has embraced this idea and created the "hip" trend of making technology seem more premium by introducing an intentional flaw: the thickness.

I have been daily-driving two devices. A Samsung Galaxy S22+ and a second generation Apple iPhone SE. With cases, the size difference is minimal, but bare, the SE is so much thinner than the S22+. Obviously this makes sense, given a higher component density, faster and newer components, and an overall larger footprint, but when you look a little closer, you see the differences could be minimal. Some of China's largest mobile device producers like Oppo are making ridiculously thin devices with higher processing power at much cheaper cost to the user. We can even see this in some of Samsung's older devices, just go back to the S20, it was almost as thin as the SE, the objective was to fit as much in as little space as possible. Making the phones boxy has also played an important role, giving more space inside, while increasing the premium feeling of the device. The iPhones made this switch in the 12th generation and the Galaxies in the 21st.

This trend isn't just limited to phones. The argument that MacBooks are "better" because their build is more durable and they feel more premium has been used ever since its modern inception. It serves as the premium option for average consumers. I remember seeing a Reel in which someone compares a MacBook Air to an HP Stream and shows that the MacBook's lid can be opened without holding the base down, and the Stream's couldn't. They also compared deck flex in the body and showed the difference in processing power. The comments were all in support of the creator and were something along the lines of, "Yes! Apple is better, just look at this irrefutable evidence!" None of them, however, took into consideration the price differences. The Stream can be purchased new regularly for under CA$300 where the MacBook Air starts at CA$1400. That is a jump of roughly 5x from the price of the Stream. With laptops everyday getting more durable and cheaper, Apple wants to maintain this cult following, and that gets harder when the competition is just as good. Apple's response to this is to make the devices thicker again, and more boxy.

The laptop I write this on, an HP Pavilion Plus, is well built with an aluminum body while still managing to be thinner than a current MacBook. Portable and sleek, it surpasses all my, and any average consumer's, needs while being thin enough to shove into a stuffed backpack with clothes for a weekend trip. My friend is going to university in the fall and he got his sister's old MacBook 2016. Comparing that to my laptop, sure it's a little thicker, it's 7 years older, but comparing that to an M1 MacBook, it's so much thinner. Apple fans have completely 180-ed so to speak, as they look down upon these thinner, less durable devices. Something else that has been disappearing is IO. Macs used to offer so much IO, even a FireWire port on some MacBooks. These wouldn't have been useful to so many, but were still included because it was useful to those using it. With the unification of everything under the USB Type-C interface, Apple has been dropping its IO left and right. This started a trend of losing IO with some Dells only having 2 Thunderbolt ports.

Tech companies are making some amazing strides in innovation and creating crazy powerful silicon and displays that look so real you could fool your average person. Simultaneously, they are creating hostile, anti-consumer practices to lock users into their ecosystem and keep them spending the most you can make them. Fewer and fewer people I know are using cases on a daily basis, because they make the phone cumbersome to hold. They are so bulky now, and this means users are spending more on device protection, usually straight from the companies. For the longest time, Samsung phones had removable backs and batteries. This allowed for users to replace them if they got too depleted or started swelling, but now the only solution is to pay a "certified" technician to replace it, or, more probably, to just bite the bullet and get a new device, another one that will have the same drawbacks and issues.

My sister was buying a laptop to replace her 2018 Microsoft Surface Book and to me, it seemed a no-brainer. A Framework laptop. It fit every requirement she had and it had some major bonuses. Framework is a consumer oriented brand that recently gained a lot of traction after Linus Sebastian of Linus Tech Tips made a nearly quarter million dollar investment in them. Their products are focused on making right-to-repair a priority for your average consumer. All of their components are replaceable right down to the IO you have. Despite all this, she was still hesitant, and ultimately pulled the trigger on a Dell XPS 13 because she liked the way the keyboard looked. My other sister was also replacing a similar laptop and purchased the same Dell XPS 13, despite my advertising the Framework laptop. I started thinking what makes these brands less appealing in the eyes of the average user and came to one conclusion.

People will follow what big brands are doing, and if that means sacrificing having the IO they want or the processing power just so they can have the same laptop as everyone else, they will. People buy iPhones not because they're the best phone, but because Apple has locked them into the ecosystem and being the "green bubble boy" is effectively making you a social pariah. Users keep going back to what they think is the best option, which is usually never the best option.

Companies won't listen to what the public "wants" because they know better. They can see what actually sells and what they need to do to maximize profits. Getting rid of the headphone jack wasn't because the users asked for it, but so they could push the sale of their new AirPods. Everything they do is a business decision to make the shareholders the most money. The only reason Apple transitioned to USB Type-C on the iPhones was because the European Union acted and forced them to change it in Europe, and it wasn't worth keeping Lightning in North America.

As long as there are consumers, anti-consumerism will exist, any way for companies to be able to make another few dollars from each user. Users just need to stop supporting these decisions, and they will stop making them. If people stopped buying the iPhone and Galaxy phone, brands would make drastic changes to draw these users back. But, alas, this will never happen and I will sit here dreaming of a more consumer-friendly world of technology.

Written in July 2025. Some of the devices have changed since.
